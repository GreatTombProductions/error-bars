Method

Where the numbers come from, what the margins mean, and the exact rules this tool applies.

What this is

The American Community Survey is a sample — it interviews a fraction of households and estimates the rest. The Census Bureau publishes a margin of error with every estimate to say how much sampling noise is in it. Most downstream products — market reports, dashboards, property platforms, ranked lists — republish the estimate and drop the margin. This tool puts the margin back.

How to read an estimate

Every value here is shown as estimate ± margin with the implied 90%-confidence range. A block-group population of 823 ± 312 means the survey cannot statistically distinguish 823 from anything roughly between 511 and 1,135 — the estimate is that uncertain.

The reliability badge follows the Census Bureau's own guidance where it exists:

  • Counts: the coefficient of variation (CV = standard error / estimate, with standard error = margin ÷ 1.645) below 15% reads as reliable; 15–30% as use-with-caution; above 30% as unreliable.
  • Medians (age, rent, home value, income): the Census publishes margins for medians but no CV guidance; the badge here is an explicitly labeled display heuristic based on the margin's size relative to the estimate. Read medians as ranges first.

Where a value is missing, the ACS publishes no estimate for that geography (small places frequently have no published values) — missing is not zero. Where a table is not released at block-group level (poverty, for example), the cell says so rather than guessing.

Cells without margins. Some cells carry no published margin — most notably the population margin for about 96% of counties (the ACS marks those margins “not applicable”). Those cells show the estimate alone and say so; no range or checker is fabricated. At block-group grain the ACS also publishes no median rent for roughly 30% of areas and no median age for ~1% — the missingness is part of the portrait, not an error to paper over.

Check a published number

Each statistic has a box for a number you found elsewhere. The rule applied:

  • Consistent if the number is within the estimate's margin (± margin), i.e. |number − estimate| ≤ margin.
  • Outside the margin if the gap is larger than the sampling error.
  • Distinguishable from zero if |estimate| > margin.

Caveat: comparing a single number to an estimate uses only the estimate's margin. If the published number is itself a survey estimate with its own margin, the correct test compares the difference to √(margin₁² + margin₂²) — both sides carry error, and the margin above understates the combined uncertainty. The tool is deliberately conservative in the other direction for rounded numbers: a rounded figure sitting exactly at the edge should be treated as inconclusive.

Coverage and caveats

  • Geography: all counties, census tracts, and block groups in the 50 states, District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico (2020 census geographies).
  • ZIP lookup: ZIP codes are approximated to counties by land-area overlap of the corresponding ZCTA (Census 2020 ZCTA–county relationship file). A ZIP that crosses county lines lists each county, primary first. Neighborhoods within a ZIP can straddle tracts — pick the tract containing your address via the Census Geocoder if precision matters.
  • Sampling error only: margins cover sampling variability. They do not cover non-sampling error (nonresponse, reporting, coverage) — the Census documents these separately.
  • Period estimate: 5-year estimates are averages over 2020–2024, not a snapshot of any single year. They are the only ACS products published for tracts and block groups.
  • Medians: a sample median's uncertainty depends on the local distribution; the published margin is the Census's own calculation. Small-area median margins can be very wide — that width is the finding, not a defect.
  • Rounding: displaying rounded values can make boundary comparisons look exact; the test uses unrounded values.
